Breath Holding Without Hyperventilation

What changes when a climber holds after normal breathing—and why that is not the same practice as hyperventilation followed by retention.

By Annie Anderson · Pranaclimb · 8-minute read · Updated 7 October 2026

One pause. Two changing gases.
During a breath hold
The starting conditions determine how the hold feels—and how safely its signals can be interpreted.

Breath holding is already part of climbing. It appears in hard lock-offs, steep body tension, dynamic movement and moments of commitment. As deliberate breathing protocols become established within sports performance, apnoea offers climbers an especially exciting training tool: it can develop composure under air hunger, explore the body’s oxygen-conserving responses and prepare breathing for short, intense movement. The key is understanding that a hold begun after normal breathing is physiologically different from one begun after deliberate hyperventilation.

The central point Holding after normal breathing preserves the rise in carbon dioxide that produces an early, useful urge to breathe. Hyperventilation lowers carbon dioxide before the hold, delays that warning and can allow oxygen to fall much further before discomfort becomes compelling.

Normal breathing and hyperventilation are not interchangeable

During any apnoea, oxygen is consumed and carbon dioxide accumulates. The difference is where the hold begins. After calm, normal breathing, carbon dioxide starts near its usual level. After rapid or deliberately deep breathing, carbon dioxide may already be abnormally low—even though arterial oxygen was close to fully saturated before the breathing began.

Starting condition After normal breathing After deliberate hyperventilation
CO₂ before the hold Near the person’s normal resting level Reduced through excessive ventilation
Urge to breathe Arrives earlier as CO₂ rises Delayed because CO₂ must rebuild
Hold duration Usually shorter Often longer—but not because more oxygen has been stored
Oxygen at the end Typically less severe desaturation in comparable serial holds Can reach a lower nadir before the normal warning becomes strong
Spleen response Repeated demanding holds can contract the spleen Research has not shown a larger contraction simply because hyperventilation was added

In a serial-apnoea study, just 15 seconds of hyperventilation lengthened the holds and delayed involuntary breathing movements, while also producing greater oxygen desaturation. It did not increase spleen contraction compared with normal breathing. This makes normal-breathing apnoea particularly interesting for training: the natural CO₂ signal remains available, allowing the athlete to practise control and composure without artificially postponing the urge to breathe.

What happens during a breath hold?

1 · CO₂ rises and O₂ falls The growing urge to breathe is strongly influenced by carbon dioxide and acidity. Oxygen continues to fall even when the urge has been delayed.
2 · The circulation redistributes Breath holding can combine cardiac slowing with sympathetic peripheral vasoconstriction. This is a coordinated oxygen-conserving response—not simply “switching on the parasympathetic nervous system.”
3 · The spleen may contract After several demanding apnoeas, the spleen can release concentrated red blood cells. Reviews report a transient haemoglobin rise of roughly 3–8 g/L in untrained people, generally returning towards baseline within minutes.
4 · The pressure response changes During intense exercise, holding can raise heart rate and blood pressure. A 2025 fixed-power sprint study found higher heart rate during the held-breath trial and higher systolic pressure immediately afterwards.

A transient rise in circulating haemoglobin is real and fascinating physiology. Repeated demanding apnoeas may also raise erythropoietin temporarily. Researchers are now working to understand how protocol design, training history and recovery might translate these acute responses into useful longer-term adaptations.

How breath holding may support athletic performance

The emerging picture is encouraging: breath holding can influence preparation and training, but its effect depends on the protocol, timing and sporting demand.

Some acute priming protocols have improved short time-to-exhaustion tests, suggesting that repeated apnoea can contribute to readiness under the right conditions. Other studies have found little additional benefit when an effective physical warm-up was already completed. This is useful rather than disappointing: it suggests that apnoea should be integrated purposefully, where its respiratory, perceptual or hypoxic–hypercapnic stimulus adds something the normal warm-up does not.

The strongest performance direction currently comes from dynamic, end-expiratory voluntary hypoventilation: repeated sprint work performed with short holds at low lung volume. Studies in runners, judoka and football players have reported improvements in repeated-sprint qualities. This is highly relevant to climbing, where hard sequences repeatedly combine movement, restricted ventilation, local muscular demand and short recovery opportunities.

Pranaclimb position Repeated apnoea without deliberate pre-hold hyperventilation is a legitimate respiratory-training method and an important performance frontier for climbing. It offers a practical way to train respiratory-discomfort tolerance, movement control under constrained breathing and selected high-intensity qualities while preserving the body’s natural CO₂ warning signal.

What this means for climbers

Brief holds can be mechanically useful

A short hold or Valsalva-like brace can stiffen the trunk and help transmit force through a hard move. That mechanical action is separate from CO₂ tolerance and from splenic contraction. All may occur around the same sequence, but they are not the same mechanism.

Air-hunger composure can be trained

Repeated exposure to a controlled rise in CO₂ may help a climber recognise respiratory discomfort without immediately becoming tense or accelerating into chaotic breathing. This is best described as composure and tolerance—not learning to ignore oxygen danger.

Starting lung volume matters

A hold after an ordinary inhalation begins with a larger oxygen store and different chest mechanics. A relaxed end-expiratory hold begins near functional residual capacity and usually creates air hunger sooner. A forceful exhale towards residual volume is another, more demanding condition. Protocols should record which was used rather than treating every “breath hold” as identical.

Climbing is the next performance question

The optimal protocol for sport climbing, bouldering and speed climbing has not yet been established. That creates a genuine Pranaclimb research opportunity: testing hold timing, movement quality, raw and Effective BR, RPE, recovery and subsequent performance in the environments where climbers actually move.

A Pranaclimb field protocol

Repeated Apnoea Without Deliberate Pre-Hold Hyperventilation

Purpose: explore respiratory-discomfort tolerance and recovery while keeping the CO₂ warning signal intact.

  1. Settle for 1–2 minutes. Breathe normally. Do not take rapid preparatory breaths or repeated oversized inhalations.
  2. Choose and record one starting condition. Use either one ordinary inhalation or a relaxed end-exhale. Keep it consistent across the session.
  3. Hold comfortably. Finish at a clear, pre-agreed stopping point—before straining, disorientation or loss of control. The objective is a repeatable training signal, not a personal record.
  4. Recover with normal breathing for about 2 minutes. Avoid dramatic recovery hyperventilation. Let breathing settle.
  5. Repeat 3–4 times. Record duration, air-hunger RPE, starting lung volume, recovery BR and any symptoms.

Research protocols often use maximal holds to provoke measurable physiology. A field skills session does not need to imitate a laboratory maximum.

What Pranaclimb can—and cannot—measure

Audio and observation can identify a breathing pause, its duration, the breathing pattern before it and the recovery that follows. These observations help correct raw BR and interpret where a climber braced, lost rhythm or recovered.

They do not directly measure blood gases, cerebral blood flow, spleen volume, haemoglobin concentration or erythropoietin. Effective BR is an operational coaching model: it makes concealed respiratory work visible without pretending to be a blood test.

One clear boundary: for independent Pranaclimb practice, perform deliberate apnoea dry, seated or lying down, in a hazard-free setting. In-water apnoea is a recognised freediving practice, but it requires specialist instruction, a trained and attentive buddy, an agreed rescue protocol and direct supervision throughout—never practise it alone. Do not perform deliberate holds while driving, at height, while climbing or belaying, or anywhere an unexpected loss of awareness could cause harm. Avoid deliberate pre-hold hyperventilation, which can delay the urge to breathe while oxygen continues to fall. Stop for dizziness, visual change, confusion, escalating tingling or loss of coordination. Longer or demanding apnoea work belongs with appropriately trained supervision; seek individual clinical advice if a health condition could alter the response.

The practical conclusion

Breath holding without hyperventilation keeps the body’s CO₂-based warning system available. It can expose a climber to controlled air hunger, evoke genuine cardiovascular and splenic responses, and form part of structured high-intensity training. It belongs within the wider movement bringing deliberate breathing into modern sports preparation, performance and recovery.

For Pranaclimb, the goal is skilled control: a clear starting condition, a purposeful hold, a repeatable recovery and meaningful measurement of what happens next.
