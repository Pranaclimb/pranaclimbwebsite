Breathing Science CO₂ & Physiology Performance Research Review 2026

Spleen Contraction,
Breath Holding &
Performance in Climbing

The human spleen can contract during apnoea, hypoxia, exercise and sympathetic stress, releasing stored red blood cells and contributing to a temporary rise in circulating haemoglobin concentration. Whether the brief breath holds and intermittent efforts of rock climbing produce a meaningful splenic response—or improve climbing performance—has not yet been tested directly.

Annie Anderson
Researcher · Breathwork Specialist · Climber
By Annie Anderson · Pranaclimb 10 min read

🫁 A Possible Mechanism During Hard Climbing

Breath holding can interrupt ventilation and may be followed by compensatory breathing. In the Pranaclimb field model, observable breath holds are therefore recorded separately and may contribute to Effective BR and estimated W′bal cost. These are operational modelling choices, not direct measurements of oxygen transport or splenic function. Laboratory research also shows that repeated maximal apnoeas can contract the spleen, but a performance benefit from strategic breath holding in climbing has not been demonstrated.

"I'd hyperventilate for two minutes and then I'd breathe out and hold my breath."
Tim Emmett — Climbing.com interview, December 2025 · Era Vella 9a

Emmett repeated that cycle five times, a couple of days before his send, holding the final exhale for over four minutes. He also credited cold water immersion for aiding his recovery through the eight-year project.

Tim's four-minute exhale hold shows exceptional breath-hold ability and an important personal relationship with breathwork. Because he performed the exercise several days before the ascent, however, the acute haemoglobin response described in apnoea studies would have resolved long before the climb. This anecdote cannot show that splenic contraction contributed to the send; any psychological, technical or longer-term training effects remain separate possibilities.

The relevant mechanism is the spleen's capacity to act as a dynamic reservoir of erythrocytes. During sufficiently strong physiological stimuli, splenic contraction can add red blood cells to the circulation and contribute to a transient increase in haemoglobin concentration. Research supports this response during repeated maximal apnoeas, hypoxia and sustained exercise. It does not yet establish the size, timing or functional importance of the response during a climbing crux. For Pranaclimb, spleen physiology is therefore best treated as an emerging hypothesis rather than a validated component of the RBA.

🩸 The Spleen: Your Body's Secret O₂ Reserve

The human spleen contains a concentrated reserve of red blood cells and can reduce its volume under physiological stress. Human spleen size varies considerably between individuals. In the studies reviewed here, mean resting volumes ranged from approximately 157 ml in untrained controls to 214 ml in elite biathletes, while well-trained cross-country skiers averaged 266 ml. These ultrasound-derived organ volumes should not be presented as equivalent to a fixed volume of releasable blood.

💧 What the Spleen Stores

A variable reserve of erythrocyte-rich blood. Baseline organ volume and the absolute volume expelled differ substantially between people.

⚡ What Triggers Release

Apnoea, hypoxia, exercise and sympathetic activation can all contribute. Hypoxaemia, hypercapnia, exercise intensity and baroreceptor unloading may influence the response.

With serial maximal apnoeas, splenic contraction and the associated haemoglobin rise develop over repeated holds and return towards baseline within minutes after the stimulus ends. The time course depends on the protocol. A transient rise in haemoglobin concentration can increase arterial oxygen content, but whether the change is large enough to improve sports performance is uncertain.

Key finding (Lindblom et al., 2024): In 14 well-trained male cross-country skiers, a serial maximal-apnoea test reduced spleen volume by 20.4% and increased haemoglobin concentration by 3.3%. The maximal roller-ski test produced a larger 35.8% contraction and an 8.1% rise in haemoglobin concentration. Exercise-related changes in plasma volume also contributed, so the haemoglobin rise cannot be attributed entirely to the spleen.

🧗 Why This Matters Specifically in Climbing

Hard climbing combines intermittent muscular work, sympathetic arousal and sometimes brief breath holds, so a splenic response is physiologically plausible. It has not yet been measured directly, and climbing cannot currently be described as uniquely favourable for splenic contraction.

The closest data on climbers specifically comes from altitude, not rock: Schagatay et al. (2020) found Mt. Everest climbers had a larger resting spleen (367 mL) than Everest Base Camp trekkers (228 mL), and roughly twice the splenic contraction during apnoea. This suggests spleen function may matter for high-altitude mountaineering, but Everest climbers are not sport or trad climbers, and the finding cannot be extended to rock climbing without direct testing.

Lodin-Sundström et al. (2026) found significant splenic contraction during head-up tilt (5.3%), a cold-pressor test (6.6%), thigh-cuff release (11.9%) and maximal-duration apnoea (9.3%). The cold-pressor test produces cold and pain as well as psychophysiological stress; it should not be labelled simply as “psychological stress”. The findings support a role for general sympathetic activation and arterial baroreceptor unloading, but they do not show that fear or arousal during climbing contracts the spleen. That remains a testable hypothesis.

Additionally, during maximal efforts climbers hold their breath for multiple reasons:

  • → Valsalva-like bracing during lock-offs, dynos, and powerful moves
  • → Trunk stabilisation to maintain body tension on small footholds
  • → Concentration and fear response — the nervous system naturally restricts breathing under stress
  • → Minimising torso movement on balance-critical sequences
Exercise intensity, repeated apnoea, hypoxia and sympathoexcitation can all influence spleen volume. The size and functional value of this response in rock climbing remain unknown.
— Evidence summary from the studies reviewed below

Holmström et al. (2021) found that elite biathletes had larger resting spleen volumes and larger absolute apnoea-induced contractions than untrained controls. Because that study was cross-sectional, it cannot determine how much of the difference reflects training, athlete selection, genetics or other factors.

🫁 The Pranaclimb cMBT — A Field Measure, Not a Spleen Test

The Pranaclimb cMBT is an exploratory, climbing-specific field assessment in which a person walks after a normal exhalation until the first clear urge to breathe. The score may reflect several influences, including starting lung volume, walking pace, familiarity, perception of respiratory discomfort and the accumulating effects of carbon dioxide and falling oxygen. It does not isolate “CO₂ tolerance”, measure spleen contraction or diagnose chemoreflex sensitivity, and its relationship with climbing performance has not yet been validated.

Pranaclimb interpretation: the cMBT provides a repeatable within-person marker of early breath-discomfort tolerance when the protocol is tightly standardised. It is best used to track an individual's change over time, alongside—not in place of—BR, RPE and HRR₆₀.

How to Perform the cMBT (Off the Wall)

  • 1 Stand upright and relaxed
  • 2 Exhale normally through the nose — do not force it
  • 3 Pinch your nose to hold the breath
  • 4 Walk at a normal, relaxed pace
  • 5 Count your steps until the first clear urge to breathe
  • 6 Stop immediately — do not push into distress

👉 The number of steps = your cMBT score. This test is about control, not suffering.

Protocol A — Pre-Climb Interpretation

🔹 What the evidence supports

Repeated maximal apnoeas can produce temporary splenic contraction and a small rise in haemoglobin concentration. They have not been shown to improve climbing performance, and previous cycling studies cited by Lindblom et al. found no performance improvement after apnoea-induced contraction.

Practical position: do not present maximal breath holds as a proven redpoint warm-up. If apnoea is assessed or trained, do it off the wall, seated or lying down, in an appropriate supervised setting—not immediately before exposed climbing.

Protocol B — Crux Management

🔹 On the wall — high-tension moves

A brief, naturally occurring breath hold may accompany bracing during a high-tension move. Record its duration, then encourage the climber to resume ventilation rather than extending the hold.

Cue: "Brace briefly if the move demands it—then release and breathe."

Protocol C — Recovery After Crux

🔹 At shake-outs, clips, and rests

At a secure rest, prioritise continuous, adequately sized breaths and a relaxed exhalation. A natural end-expiratory pause is acceptable, but there is no evidence here that deliberate recovery holds accelerate HRR₆₀ or W′bal reconstitution.

Cue: "Let the exhale soften—then allow the next breath to arrive without forcing or delaying it."

🔗 How This Fits the Pranaclimb Stack

🫁 cMBT (off wall)

Provides a standardised, within-person marker of early breath-discomfort tolerance.

📊 BR + RPE (on wall)

Show how fast you approach your limit — how quickly the container fills.

😤 Expressive breathing (on wall)

Shows how you cope when you are near the limit — grunts, screams, holds.

❤️ HRR₆₀ (post wall)

Shows how well you recover afterward — and how fast the container empties.

"The Pranaclimb cMBT is performed off the wall as an exploratory field measure and interpreted alongside BR, RPE and HRR₆₀—not as an isolated measure of CO₂ tolerance or spleen function."
— Pranaclimb

⚠️ Safety: Never practise breath holds in water, while driving, standing near an edge, on the climbing wall or anywhere a loss of consciousness could cause injury.

⚠️ Do not hyperventilate before a breath hold. Stop for dizziness, visual changes, confusion, chest pain or unusual distress. A pulse oximeter does not prevent blackout.

⚠️ People with cardiovascular, respiratory, neurological or relevant medical conditions—and anyone who is pregnant—should seek individual clinical advice before deliberate apnoea training. Longer or maximal apnoeas require appropriately qualified supervision.
