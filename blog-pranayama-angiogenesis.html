Breathwork Recovery Physiology

Pranayama, Nitric Oxide
& Vascular Adaptation

Humming can markedly increase nasal nitric-oxide output during the practice, while sufficiently demanding apnoea can alter blood gases and hypoxic signalling. These are credible connections to vascular physiology—but they do not yet prove that pranayama independently grows new blood vessels or improves climbing performance.

Annie Anderson
Researcher · Breathwork Specialist · Climber
By Annie Anderson 7 min read

What Is Angiogenesis?

Angiogenesis is the formation of new capillaries from existing vessels. The resulting capillary supply can support oxygen diffusion and the exchange of nutrients and metabolites in active muscle. Exercise training is an established stimulus for skeletal-muscle capillarisation; the response depends on the muscle being trained, the exercise dose and the athlete's starting condition.

The question for Pranaclimb is more specific: can a breathing practice add a meaningful vascular stimulus of its own? That is biologically plausible for some sufficiently demanding hypoxic or movement-apnoea protocols, but it has not been demonstrated for ordinary slow pranayama or humming in climbers.

The controlled position: climbing and other muscular training provide the primary angiogenic stimulus. Breathwork may support training quality, respiratory control and recovery; repeated hypoxic practice is an emerging research direction rather than a proven shortcut to new capillaries.

1. Breath Holds, Hypoxia & HIF Signalling

Hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) help coordinate cellular responses when oxygen availability falls. In exercise research, repeated muscular work under systemic hypoxia can strengthen angiogenic signalling and, in some training studies, capillary adaptation.

Not every breath hold creates the same stimulus. A short comfortable retention may produce little hypoxaemia, whereas repeated or prolonged apnoea—especially with movement—can progressively lower oxygen and raise carbon dioxide. The dose, lung volume, preceding breathing and muscular work all matter. Nadi Shodhana, quiet slow breathing and demanding Kumbhaka should therefore not be treated as physiologically interchangeable.

"A pathway being activated is not the same as a new capillary being formed. Pranaclimb separates the acute signal from the long-term structural adaptation."
— Pranaclimb evidence boundary

🐝 Good Vibrations: Bhramari & Nitric Oxide

Bhramari, the humming-bee breath, has a clear acute physiological effect. In ten healthy participants, humming increased measured nasal nitric oxide approximately 15-fold compared with quiet exhalation at the same flow. The authors attributed this mainly to oscillating airflow improving gas exchange between the paranasal sinuses and nasal cavity.

This is useful evidence for the mechanism of humming, but the measurement was nasal NO during a single-breath laboratory manoeuvre. The study did not test muscle blood flow, angiogenesis, CO₂ tolerance, climbing performance or respiratory fatigue. Bhramari remains valuable as a paced, audible exhalation and attentional practice without needing those additional claims.

The key distinction: Bhramari uses a slow humming exhale and acutely increases nasal NO exchange. Bhastrika is a rapid ventilatory practice with a different arousal and blood-gas profile. They should be programmed for different purposes rather than grouped together as one “angiogenesis” intervention.

Benefits of Bhramari for Climbers

🩸 Nitric Oxide ×15

Humming produced approximately 15-fold higher nasal NO than quiet exhalation in a small laboratory study. This is a local airway measurement, not proof of increased muscle oxygen delivery.

🌬️ CO₂ Tolerance

A prolonged humming exhale provides rhythm and mild expiratory resistance. Whether Bhramari itself produces a meaningful CO₂-tolerance adaptation has not been established.

🧘 Stress & Anxiety Relief

The sound and extended exhale can provide an attentional anchor and a practical downshift between attempts. Individual responses should be observed rather than assumed.

💨 Active Exhale Cue

The buzzing sound makes the exhale audible and easy to pace, helping the climber practise a smooth, unforced release.

How to Practise Bhramari

🐝 Begin with 5 minutes

  • 1️⃣ Sit in a crossed-legged position, spine straight
  • 2️⃣ Close your eyes to calm and internalise consciousness
  • 3️⃣ Place thumbs in ears to close off hearing
  • 4️⃣ Place fingers over eyes, bridge of nose, pinkies touching lips
  • 5️⃣ Inhale slowly and deeply through the nostrils
  • 6️⃣ Exhale making a bumblebee humming sound — lips sealed
"Use the hum for what it gives you directly: an audible, extended exhale, an attentional anchor and a measurable increase in nasal nitric-oxide exchange during the practice."
— Pranaclimb

Nitric oxide participates in vascular regulation and exercise-related signalling, but nasal NO output is not a direct measurement of systemic NO availability or new vessel growth. The defensible interpretation is therefore narrower:

  • → Humming changes sinus ventilation and nasal NO exchange acutely
  • → A long, controlled exhale can support pacing and attention
  • → Exercise remains the established driver of muscle capillarisation
  • → Transfer to forearm perfusion and climbing performance still requires direct testing

3. Regulation & Recovery

Slow pranayama can influence breathing rate, attention and autonomic state. The response is pattern- and person-dependent, so Pranaclimb avoids describing every slow breath as simply “switching on the parasympathetic nervous system.” For climbers, the practical value is the ability to create a repeatable rhythm before an attempt or during recovery.

🧠 Attention & Composure

The audible vibration and extended exhale provide a simple sensory anchor that may help interrupt hurried breathing and redirect attention.

❤️ Observe Recovery

Pranaclimb can record post-effort BR, HRR₆₀ and RPE before and after a chosen recovery practice. These field measures describe the response; they do not prove angiogenesis or W′bal recharge.

The Pranaclimb Perspective

The Pranaclimb Methodology gives these practices distinct jobs. Bhramari is an audible extended-exhale and attention practice. Slow pranayama supports regulation and breathing awareness. Repeated apnoea is a separate hypoxic–hypercapnic training question. Climbing volume and conditioning remain the central stimulus for forearm vascular adaptation.

Key Takeaway: Humming has a measurable nasal nitric-oxide effect, and demanding apnoea can create a hypoxic stimulus. Whether pranayama independently grows new blood vessels in climbers remains unproven. Train it for the breathing skill it develops, and test any performance transfer transparently.
