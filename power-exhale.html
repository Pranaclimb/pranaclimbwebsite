Power Exhale

A short, intentional exhale timed to a difficult movement—helping the climber express force without extending a closed-glottis strain.

Pranaclimb Position

How Climbers Can Use This Technique

Pranaclimb uses the Power Exhale as a movement-timing cue: prepare, move and release a brief “ha” through the effort. Peak Expiratory Flow Rate (PEFR) can sit beside it as an optional off-wall Power marker. The intervention and measurement are related, but not interchangeable: a peak-flow score does not tell us whether the exhale was timed effectively on the wall.
Mechanism & Coaching

The Breathing Science

  • Movement timing: an audible exhale gives the climber and coach a clear marker of when force is expressed.
  • Pressure release: opening the airway distinguishes the Power Exhale from a prolonged closed-glottis Valsalva manoeuvre.
  • Force production: forced exhalation increased maximal force in some tested isometric actions, while Valsalva showed no advantage over forced exhalation. This is supportive—not climbing-specific—evidence.
  • PEFR: peak expiratory flow records the fastest airflow reached during a short maximal exhalation from full inspiration. It is effort- and technique-dependent and is influenced by airway calibre, lung volume and expiratory muscle contribution.
Exact Intervention

Choose The Right Dose

Ground rehearsal

6 repetitions

Brace gently, make one sharp “ha” with a safe push or pull, then take two quiet recovery breaths. Test PEFR separately when fresh—not on every repetition.

Easy climbing

4–6 moves

Pair one exhale with one familiar, well-protected movement. Release fully; do not stack repeated forced exhales.

Crux application

One selected move

Choose the movement in advance. Use the cue only if it improves timing and does not disrupt the movement solution.

Timing

When To Use It For Climbing

  • Dynamic reaches, deadpoints or powerful lock-offs
  • Rehearsed cruxes where an audible timing cue helps
  • Not as a mandatory rhythm for every move
Field Measurement

What To Record

On the wall, record the move, Raw BR, Effective BR, RPE, whether a hold preceded the exhale and the next recovery breaths. Off the wall, PEFR can be recorded as the best of three technically valid efforts and compared with the climber’s own baseline. An audible exhale does not quantify intrathoracic pressure, PEFR or W′ expenditure.

Optional Power Marker

PEFR: Test The Exhale Off The Wall

Peak Expiratory Flow Rate (PEFR) measures how quickly air can be expelled from the lungs, in litres per minute. It is used clinically to monitor airflow—especially in asthma—and is also affected by starting lung volume, technique and voluntary effort. Pranaclimb therefore treats PEFR as an optional contextual marker, not a diagnosis or a standalone measure of diaphragm strength.

  1. Use the same Mini-Wright meter, posture and approximate time of day. Test when fresh, before Power Exhale training.
  2. Reset the pointer. Inhale fully, seal the lips around the mouthpiece, then deliver one short, hard and fast blow.
  3. Record the value, rest until breathing feels normal and repeat for three trials. Retain the best technically valid reading.
  4. Compare with your personal baseline and relevant clinical reference values. Retest every 4–6 weeks—not repeatedly within a training drill.

Research

Climbing Application, Evidence & Limits

Breathing and force

In ten participants, forced exhalation increased peak force in shoulder adduction, elbow extension and knee extension but not every tested action. Valsalva was not superior.

Ikeda et al., 2009 →
Climbing pressure

Maximal bouldering produced substantial mouth-pressure and cardiovascular responses in six trained male climbers, supporting the presence of Valsalva-like strain during hard climbing.

Callender et al., 2020 →
PEFR and abdominal contribution

In 23 healthy men, peak expiratory flow was correlated with abdominal muscle thickness during forced expiration. This supports an expiratory-muscle contribution but does not make PEFR a direct core-strength or climbing-performance test.

Safety Boundary

Practise In Control

Stop before strain: Rehearse first on the ground or on easy, familiar, securely protected climbing. Avoid prolonged maximal straining. Stop for dizziness, chest pain, visual disturbance or unusual headache. Climbers with cardiovascular or blood-pressure concerns should obtain appropriate clinical advice.
