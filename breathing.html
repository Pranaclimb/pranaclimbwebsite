Activation
🌬
Wim Hof–Style Breathing
Controlled stress · Activation & focus

A powerful cycle of deep rhythmic breathing, low-lung-volume retention and a recovery breath. It deliberately changes blood gases, activates the sympathetic nervous system and creates a controlled hypoxic challenge—training focus, commitment and familiarity with respiratory discomfort.

"Breathe with purpose. Hold with awareness. Return in control."

Explore the method & science

The breathing cycle: 30–40 deep rhythmic breaths reduce CO₂ and temporarily raise blood pH. The relaxed exhalation retention then allows O₂ to fall and CO₂ to rise again, creating a distinct hypoxic and interoceptive challenge. One recovery breath completes the round.

Why climbers may use it: the practice combines activation with voluntary control. It can provide an intense rehearsal of focus, commitment and composure while respiratory sensations change—qualities that may transfer meaningfully to the psychological demands of hard climbing.

What research has observed: trained practitioners have demonstrated voluntary sympathetic activation and modulation of the innate immune response. A small study in young endurance athletes also reported faster VO₂ kinetics and lower perceived strain after a 17–22-minute breathing warm-up. These findings give the method a credible physiological and performance rationale, while direct climbing and Critical Power effects still require testing.

The Pranaclimb position: Wim Hof–style breathing is a purposeful off-wall activation and controlled-stress practice. We use it to explore readiness, respiratory composure and volitional control—not as a breathing pattern to perform while climbing.

Evidence and further reading: Kox et al. · Bahenský et al. · Citherlet et al. · systematic review · Oxygen Advantage overview

Choose your guided practice:

Beginner: official three-round guided introduction, approximately 11 minutes. Practise seated or lying down in a safe place and let every retention remain comfortable.

Recovery
⬜
Box Breathing
Regulation · Focus under pressure

An equal 4:4:4:4 cycle of inhalation, retention, exhalation and retention. The predictable rhythm anchors attention and helps regulate arousal before climbing, during route reading, at secure rests and between attempts.

"Follow the square. Steady the breath. Regain control."

Explore the practice & science

The practice: inhale gently through the nose for 4 seconds while tracing the first side of a square. Hold for 4 seconds at the corner. Exhale steadily through the nose or mouth for 4 seconds along the next side. Hold after the exhale for 4 seconds to complete the square. One full cycle takes 16 seconds—approximately 3.75 breaths per minute.

Why climbers use it: the repeating count provides a clear attentional task when stress makes breathing and decision-making feel chaotic. In Pranaclimb it is a regulation practice—not a maximal breathing pattern and not a breathing rhythm that must be maintained through hard continuous movement.

What the evidence shows: tactical breathing is widely taught for managing arousal under pressure. Experimental evidence indicates that it can reduce physiological arousal, although longer-exhale breathing may be more effective for some performance and HRV outcomes. Daily five-minute breathwork has also improved positive affect and respiratory-rate regulation, with cyclic sighing producing the strongest effects in the comparative trial.

CO₂ and W′bal: the pauses alter CO₂ dynamics, but Box Breathing does not reliably increase CO₂; the response depends on breath depth and total ventilation. Keep each breath quiet and comfortable rather than oversized. The practice can support composure and ventilatory control while physiological recovery and W′bal reconstitution occur—it does not directly restore or measure W′bal.

Where it fits: use it before starting, while visualising or reading a route, between attempts, or at a secure rest. During difficult continuous climbing, return to unrestricted breathing whenever the four-second pauses interfere with movement or create strain.

Emerging evidence: a small 30-day study reported improvements in FVC, FEV₁ and FIVC, but this finding is preliminary and requires replication before lung-function improvement can be treated as an established benefit.

Evidence and further reading: Marchant et al. · Balban et al. · Röttger et al. · Fincham et al. · Ahmed et al.

Recovery
💨
Cyclic Sighing
Emotion regulation · Promising evidence

A gentle nasal inhale, a shorter nasal top-up and a relaxed extended exhale. Use one breath, one minute or five minutes to pause, release tension and refocus.

“Pause. Release. Refocus.”

Explore the practice & science

The practice: inhale gently through the nose, add one shorter nasal sip without exhaling, then release a slow, unforced exhale through the mouth.

Pranaclimb application: use one sigh before leaving the ground or at a secure rest; one minute between attempts; or five minutes before or after climbing and as a daily practice.

What the evidence shows: in a remote month-long trial, five minutes of daily cyclic sighing produced the strongest positive-affect and resting-respiratory-rate results among the tested breathwork conditions. Later evidence is encouraging but mixed, so the technique should not be described as a universal parasympathetic switch or a treatment for anxiety.

Balloon Breathing
Resisted exhale · Coaching intervention

A gentle nasal inhale followed by a smooth, non-maximal exhale into a balloon. It trains expiratory control and diaphragm–abdominal coordination. A clinical study reported an acute SpO₂ rise in hospitalised COVID-19 patients; transfer to healthy climbers remains a Pranaclimb field question.

"Steady pressure. Soft face. No strain."

Research Spotlight

The science behind it

Wim Hof · VO₂ Kinetics

Warm-Up Breathing & Endurance

A small study in young endurance athletes reported altered VO₂ kinetics and lower perceived strain after a 17–22-minute Wim Hof–style breathing warm-up. It did not demonstrate a meaningful performance improvement, and the proposed mechanisms remain speculative.

Bahenský et al. · Preliminary

Cyclic Sighing · Mood

Five-Minute Daily Practice

In a month-long randomised trial, exhale-focused cyclic sighing produced greater improvement in positive affect and a larger reduction in resting respiratory rate than mindfulness in the reported models. Later findings are promising but mixed.

Balban et al., 2023 · Evidence evolving

Box Breathing · Regulation

Structure, Arousal & Attention

Box Breathing provides a repeatable attentional rhythm and can reduce physiological arousal under stress. It is not the strongest established HRV protocol: in an 84-person comparison, 6-breaths-per-minute breathing increased HRV more than square breathing.

Röttger et al., 2021 · Marchant et al., 2025

Balloon Breathing · Evidence Boundary

Resisted Exhale, Not an Oxygen Score

Misra et al. reported mean SpO₂ rising from 95.7% to 98.1% after 30 minutes in 250 hospitalised COVID-19 patients. The result supports an acute clinical effect in that population—not a guaranteed response or climbing-performance gain in healthy athletes.

Misra et al., 2023 · population-specific evidence

Vocalisation · Ventilation

Yelling & Aerobic Power

Yelling at maximal effort led to higher peak power, time to exhaustion, and greater VO₂ and ventilation during 90–100% maximal cycling — without increased muscle activation. Supports Pranaclimb's +5 BPM scream adjustment rule.

Chen et al., 2015 — ResearchGate

Power Exhale · Force Expression

A Brief Alternative to Prolonged Strain

Forced exhalation increased maximal force in some—not all—tested isometric actions, and Valsalva was not superior. Optional PEFR records maximal expiratory flow off the wall; it complements the movement cue but does not measure crux timing or climbing performance.

Ikeda et al., 2009 · Ishida et al., 2014

Sighing · Reset

Sighs as Physiological Resetters

Spontaneous sighs act as physiological and psychological resetters, reducing tension and improving self-reported relief. Supports assigning a low or negative BR adjustment for sighs in Pranaclimb coaching.

Vlemincx et al., 2016

🎈 Balloon Breathing

Expansion Breath · Recovery Zone

What it is: Breathe deeply through the nose, then exhale slowly and steadily into a balloon, extending the exhalation as long as possible.

Why it works: Balloon resistance provides feedback about expiratory pressure and may help coordinate the diaphragm, abdominal wall and ribcage. Misra et al. reported an acute SpO₂ rise from 95.7% to 98.1% in hospitalised COVID-19 patients; that result is population-specific, not a promised response in healthy climbers.

When to use: Recovery phases, off-wall sessions, between redpoint attempts.

"Steady pressure. Soft face. No strain."

Research: Misra et al. (2023) reported an acute SpO₂ increase after 30 minutes in 250 hospitalised COVID-19 patients. Boyle et al. (2010) provide the diaphragm–abdominal coordination rationale. Neither study establishes a climbing-performance benefit.

⚡ Power Exhale

Crux Tool · Optional PEFR Power Marker

What it is: A forceful, sharp exhale timed to the hardest move on the wall. Think of it as a controlled burst — not a scream, but a deliberate pressure release.

Why it works: The audible exhale creates a clear movement-timing marker and opens the airway rather than extending a closed-glottis strain. Forced exhalation increased force in some tested isometric actions, while Valsalva was not superior (Ikeda et al., 2009).

Optional PEFR: test Peak Expiratory Flow Rate off the wall with a Mini-Wright meter when fresh. Take three technically valid efforts after full inspiration, record the best in L/min and compare it with the climber’s personal baseline. PEFR reflects airflow, technique and effort; it is not a direct measure of Power Exhale timing, diaphragm strength or W′.

When to use: At crux moves, hard lock-offs, or when you feel the pump onset. Not for continuous use — use once, then reset with nasal breathing.

"Exhale sharp — drive through the crux."

Pranaclimb measurement: record the audible exhale, any preceding breath hold, Raw BR, Effective BR and the following recovery breaths. Do not infer airway pressure or W′ expenditure from the sound alone.

👅 Tongue and Jaw Reset

Recovery Cue · Below CP

What it is: Let the tongue rest lightly near the front of the hard palate without pressing. Keep the lips soft, teeth slightly apart and jaw free. Allow quiet nasal breathing where comfortable.

Why it may help: The cue makes jaw clenching, facial effort and neck tension easier to notice and gives the climber a repeatable route back to a comfortable breath. Its value should be judged from the climber’s response.

When to use: During route reading, between attempts, at a secure below-CP recovery hold or after a crux. Do not impose the position during hard movement.

"Tongue light, teeth apart, jaw free—let the breath settle."

Evidence boundary: Active tongue pressure can alter jaw and deep-neck muscle activity, but research does not show one universal relaxation response or a direct climbing-performance benefit. Pranaclimb therefore uses this as a light awareness cue and tests the individual response.
