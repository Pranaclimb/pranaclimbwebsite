Breath Holding in Climbing:
Brace. Move. Breathe.

Climbers often interrupt breathing at the hardest point of a movement. This is not necessarily an error: the hold may include a brief Valsalva-like strain that raises thoracoabdominal pressure, increases trunk stiffness and supports force transmission between the limbs. When precisely timed, it can help the climber brace and execute a maximal move. If prolonged or repeated, however, it suppresses ventilation and increases cardiovascular and respiratory strain. The skill is therefore not eliminating every breath hold, but using it briefly and releasing promptly back into an effective breathing rhythm.

Pranaclimb position: breath holding is a trainable climbing behaviour. We develop the useful hold, the timely release and the recovery breath—then observe what changes on the wall.

Breath Holding Is Already Part of Hard Climbing

Watch a climber at absolute maximum. During a lock-off, deadpoint, heel-hook or powerful boulder sequence, breathing may briefly stop. The torso stiffens, force transfers through the body and attention narrows onto the move.

The important question is not simply, “Did the climber hold their breath?” It is: Did the hold help the movement, and did breathing restart soon enough to protect the next move?

The Valsalva: More Than Simply Holding Your Breath

A Valsalva manoeuvre is a forceful attempt to breathe out against a closed airway. During a hard lock-off, compression move or dynamic reach, a climber may create a brief Valsalva-like strain without consciously planning it. The rise in pressure through the chest and abdomen can help stiffen the trunk and transfer force across the body.

That pressure also changes circulation. If the strain continues, venous return and cardiac output can fall while the sympathetic nervous system works to defend blood pressure. When the hold is released, blood pressure can briefly dip and then overshoot. This is why Pranaclimb treats the hold as a useful but dose-dependent performance tool: brace for the move, release promptly, and restore breathing rather than stacking one hold onto the next.

In six trained male climbers, indoor bouldering produced intermittent mouth-pressure changes consistent with Valsalva-like effort and a substantial blood-pressure response. Separate strength research found that forced exhalation increased peak force in some muscle actions and that a full Valsalva was not superior. Together, these findings support training both options: a very brief hold when maximal stiffness is needed, and a forceful exhale when the move can be completed without closing the airway.

The convergence: one maximal move can contain four connected layers. You see the breath hold; Valsalva-like pressure may help create the brace; CO₂ tolerance influences how well the climber manages rising air hunger; and sufficiently strong apnoea and exercise stress may also engage the spleen. They can work together, but they are not four names for the same response.

Where the Spleen Fits

The spleen stores red-blood-cell-rich blood. During demanding apnoea, hypoxia and intense exercise it can contract, releasing some of those cells into circulation and temporarily increasing haemoglobin concentration.

That makes the spleen relevant to the Pranaclimb model. Maximum-intensity climbing combines muscular work, sympathetic stress and frequent breath holds. But a visible hold does not prove that the spleen has contracted, and spleen contraction has not yet been isolated as the reason a climber completes a route.

Pranaclimb therefore treats the spleen as a credible contributing mechanism within a larger performance response—not as a magic switch.

Sport Routes and Boulders Need Different Breath Strategies

Sport-route onsight

The effort is longer and recovery matters. Brief holds may help at individual crux moves, but the priority is restarting the breath, using rests well and preventing one hard sequence from disrupting the rest of the route.

Boulder onsight

The effort is shorter and more explosive. A deliberate brace may link two or three maximal moves. The priority is precision through the sequence, followed by an immediate release and full recovery.

How Pranaclimb Trains It

1. Off-wall CO₂-related practice

Use four comfortable end-expiratory walking holds of roughly 15–25 steps, with full breathing recovery between rounds. The aim is composure and control, not maximal deprivation.

2. Sport-climbing practice

On easy, familiar and securely protected climbing, explore a brief 2–3-second hold every 5–10 breaths. Release cleanly and check that rhythm, accuracy and decision-making remain intact.

3. Boulder practice

On low-risk familiar terrain, climb a controlled two- or three-move sequence on one breath. Match the release to the end of the sequence, then use about one minute of calm recovery breathing.

During a Respiratory Breathing Assessment, Pranaclimb can record hold duration, Raw Breathing Rate, Effective Breathing Rate, RPE and recovery. This turns an invisible habit into a behaviour that can be compared across attempts.

Elite-climbing observation

Tim Emmett used demanding breath-retention practice before climbing Era Vella 9a. Because the practice occurred a couple of days before the ascent, it cannot have supplied a short-lived red-cell boost during the send. Its more plausible contribution was longer-term familiarity with respiratory discomfort, composure under strain and confidence in continuing when breathing pressure became intense.

What We Know—and What We Are Testing

Research already shows that repeated apnoea and intense exercise can provoke meaningful physiological responses. Exercise breath-hold training has also improved the maintenance of later repeated high-intensity efforts in elite athletes.

The climbing-specific protocol is the next question. Pranaclimb is testing how hold duration, movement type, recovery breathing and the athlete’s individual response interact on real climbing. The method is controlled, progressive and measurable while the science continues to develop.

Safety: brief planned holds on easy, familiar, securely protected climbing are different from maximal or serial apnoea. Practise demanding breath holds dry, at ground level and with appropriate supervision. Do not hyperventilate before breath holding in water, while driving, belaying or on exposed terrain.
