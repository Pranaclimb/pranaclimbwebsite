The Science of Power Screaming

Why climbers yell on hard moves: acute force, breath–movement coupling, respiratory mechanics, expression — and what the scream can tell us about Raw BR and Effective BR.

Annie Anderson
Founder, Pranaclimb · Independent Researcher
Updated September 2026  ·  10 min read

Key Points: The Science Behind the Power Scream

  • ~25% greater handgrip force: vocalised exhalation produced greater handgrip force than passive breathing in Rodolico et al.; this was a 30-participant conference-proceedings study.
  • ~6% higher peak cycling power: Chen et al. reported 185.0 ± 5.7 W with yelling versus 174.5 ± 7.0 W in control.
  • ~5.8% longer time to exhaustion: 478.0 ± 14.0 s with yelling versus 451.9 ± 16.6 s in control in Chen et al.
  • Respiratory rate increased by ~7.6 breaths/min: 38.57 ± 9.69 to 46.17 ± 12.18 breaths/min at maximal effort in Chen et al.
  • Minute ventilation increased: 58.70 ± 20.91 to 72.71 ± 25.56 L/min at maximal effort in the yelling condition.
  • The yells were abdominal: Chen et al. used 3–5 short, forceful abdominal yells and rectus-abdominis EMG to confirm abdominal involvement.
  • Breathing itself has an energetic cost: respiratory muscles may account for ~10% of whole-body O₂ consumption at maximal exercise in healthy people (Aliverti).
  • Pranaclimb distinction: Raw BR is measured · Breath Expressions are observed and classified · Effective BR is calculated.

These experimental findings provide physiological context; they do not establish that yelling produces the same effects in rock climbing, nor do they validate Pranaclimb's numerical Effective BR coefficients.

Breath, Movement and the Power Scream

A power scream is more than noise. It is breath, movement, pressure, power and expression — happening at the same time.

Watch climbers at their absolute limit and you will hear it: a scream, a grunt, a sharp hiss, a forceful exhale. Chris Sharma has his distinctive passat — ptsssaa. Adam Ondra has his unmistakable power scream.

The important feature may not simply be the sound. It is what the climber is doing with the breath at the same moment as the movement.

A powerful audible exhalation can be timed precisely with a move requiring an explosion of force or power. It may also provide immediate auditory feedback: the climber can hear that they are breathing rather than unconsciously holding their breath.

“It’s more that I need to focus to do a certain move while breathing out. If I scream while doing that move, then I’m a hundred percent sure that I’m doing that move while breathing out.”
Adam Ondra

Ondra's description is almost a climber's account of breath–movement coupling: the scream becomes an audible marker that expiration and maximal movement are occurring together.

Lynn Hill describes discovering the same relationship between breathing and movement:

“By observing my breathing patterns I discovered that while reaching in stretched-out positions, it was helpful to inhale in order to gain extra lift, and conversely, while making powerful or dynamic moves, it was helpful to exhale air in a quick burst or to make a karate-style grunt.”
Lynn Hill · Climbing Free

Breath and movement are coupled

Climbing is a movement and breathing system. Our breathing muscles do not operate separately from movement. The diaphragm, rib-cage muscles and abdominal muscles participate in generating and regulating thoracic and abdominal pressures, while several of these muscles also contribute to postural control and trunk stability.

As climbing intensity rises, this relationship becomes increasingly important. A hard move may simultaneously require finger force + pulling force + trunk stiffness + pressure regulation + expiration + attention + commitment.

And sometimes that expiration becomes a scream.

Can a Power Scream Make You Stronger?

There is experimental evidence that vocalised exhalation can increase acute force production. Rodolico and colleagues compared maximal static handgrip force during passive breathing, forced exhalation and vocalised exhalation.

ConditionAverage handgrip force
Passive breathing201.2 N
Forced exhalation225.5 N
Vocalised exhalation251.2 N

Vocalised exhalation increased average static handgrip force by approximately 25% compared with passive breathing and 11% compared with forced exhalation. This was a conference-proceedings study involving 30 participants rather than a large definitive trial, so it should not be interpreted as showing that every scream makes every athlete 25% stronger. But it provides experimental evidence that the way we exhale during maximal force production can matter.

A separate experiment by Welch and Tschampl studied 50 martial artists — 25 experts and 25 novices — performing handgrip tests with and without a kiap. Handgrip performance was significantly greater with the kiap: 437.1 ± 94.9 N versus 408.0 ± 90.6 N without it.

So there really is something to shout about. Vocalisation can accompany an acute increase in force output, although the size and relevance of the effect will depend on the task and context.

What Happens to Breathing When We Yell?

Chen et al., in Effect of yelling on maximal aerobic power during an incremental test of cycling performance, studied 23 moderately trained participants during maximal incremental cycling. Participants completed a control trial and a yelling trial.

Yelling was initiated near exhaustion when participants either began to feel fatigued or met the study's physiological criterion. Participants produced 3–5 short, forceful abdominal yells, each lasting approximately 1–2 seconds and separated by 1–3 seconds.

These were not simply unspecified noises from the throat. Participants were instructed to produce the yell by forcefully contracting the abdominal muscles while emitting a short, loud “Er”. Crucially, EMG was applied to the rectus abdominis to ensure that the yells were not produced only from the throat. Participants practised the manoeuvre beforehand to ensure appropriate abdominal involvement.

That methodological detail matters for Pranaclimb. Chen deliberately distinguished a throat-only sound from an abdominally generated respiratory-vocal behaviour.

Yelling Changed Performance — and Breathing

The yelling condition produced striking differences. Peak power increased from 174.5 ± 7.0 W to 185.0 ± 5.7 W — approximately 6% — and time to exhaustion increased by approximately 5.8%.

During the final 90–100% of maximal effort there were also significant yelling-related changes in VO₂, VCO₂, O₂ pulse, minute ventilation and respiratory rate.

At maximal effortControlYelling
Respiratory rate38.57 ± 9.69 breaths/min46.17 ± 12.18 breaths/min
Minute ventilation58.70 ± 20.91 L/min72.71 ± 25.56 L/min

Interestingly, the interaction for tidal volume was not significant.

Yelling altered respiratory frequency and overall ventilation rather than simply producing a larger tidal breath.
Pranaclimb interpretation of Chen et al.

And Then There Is the 7.6 BPM Difference

The difference between the mean maximal respiratory rates was approximately 7.6 breaths/min. That is particularly interesting for the Pranaclimb Methodology because Pranaclimb currently uses an operational +5 BPM coefficient for a scream/yell when deriving Effective BR.

But this distinction is essential: the Pranaclimb +5 BPM adjustment cannot be derived from Chen's 7.6-BPM group difference. The two values are not mathematically equivalent, and Chen's experiment was not designed to determine a breathing-rate adjustment coefficient.

However, it is notable that the respiratory perturbation associated with yelling was on the order of several breaths per minute rather than being trivial. The finding therefore provides physiological context for the magnitude of the Pranaclimb operational coefficient. It does not validate it.

The +5 BPM scream/yell coefficient remains a Pranaclimb operational parameter requiring prospective validation.

The Scream Is Mechanical Too

Chen becomes even more interesting when considered alongside Aliverti's work on respiratory muscles during exercise. Aliverti describes the diaphragm, rib-cage muscles and abdominal muscles as functional components of the ventilatory pump.

At rest, expiration is largely passive. During exercise, however, expiratory muscles become actively involved. The abdominal muscles act primarily as pressure generators, while the diaphragm acts predominantly as a flow generator during exercise.

The respiratory act occurring during intense exercise therefore has a mechanical muscular component that is not represented by respiratory frequency alone. A forceful abdominal expiration or vocalisation may occur within the respiratory pattern, but counting respiratory cycles alone does not describe the character or muscular expression of that event.

And that brings us straight back to Chen: the participants did not merely make a noise. They deliberately recruited their abdominal muscles during the yell, and the researchers monitored rectus-abdominis activity to verify that involvement.

A forceful yell can therefore combine expiration + abdominal recruitment + pressure generation + phonation + movement + effort + behavioural expression.

The scream is simultaneously respiratory, abdominal/mechanical, and behavioural.
The Pranaclimb Methodology

Breathing Is Part of the Work of Exercise

Respiratory muscles themselves perform work. Aliverti reports that at maximal exercise the respiratory muscles can account for approximately 10% of whole-body oxygen consumption in healthy subjects. Research into respiratory limitations during sustained high-intensity exercise also shows that substantial respiratory-muscle work and fatigue can influence exercise performance and cardiovascular responses.

This does not mean that an individual climbing scream causes respiratory-muscle fatigue or meaningfully reduces blood flow to the forearms. The available evidence does not establish that.

It does tell us something important: breathing is part of the work of exercise. A power scream can therefore be considered both a potentially useful performance behaviour and part of the respiratory-mechanical response to intense movement.

Power, Movement and Awareness

The laboratory findings become even more interesting when we return to climbers. Ondra describes screaming to make certain he is breathing out through the move. Hill describes a rapid exhalation or karate-style grunt during powerful and dynamic movement. Chris Sharma's passat provides another example of a forceful audible expiration timed with effort.

Hazel Findlay has described a power scream helping her stay on the rock during an extremely demanding climb. Paige Claassen describes another possible function of audible breathing: feedback. Hearing the breath can help make it obvious when breathing stops and provide an attentional anchor during difficult climbing.

From Raw BR to Breath Expressions

This distinction is fundamental to the Pranaclimb Methodology.

Imagine two climbers both displaying a Raw BR of 30 breaths/min. One is breathing continuously. The other is producing breath holds, powerful expirations, grunts and screams during maximal moves.

Their observed respiratory frequencies may be identical. Their respiratory behaviours are not.

Raw BR remains essential: it tells us how frequently respiratory cycles are occurring. But respiratory frequency alone does not describe the mechanical character of those respiratory events. A normal expiration and a forceful abdominal expiration may each occur within a respiratory cycle, but they are not necessarily equivalent respiratory behaviours.

Breath Expressions aren't corrections for bad Raw BR data. They are additional observable information contained within the respiratory behaviour.

Pranaclimb therefore records observable Breath Expressions such as breath holds, grunts, screams/yells, strong inhalations and expressive expirations separately from Raw BR. Those observations can then contribute to the derived Effective Breathing Rate — Effective BR.

What Is Effective BR?

Effective BR does not claim that a scream literally equals five extra breaths. Nor is it intended to reconstruct breaths that supposedly “would have occurred.”

Effective BR is an expression-adjusted field intensity index designed to prevent Raw BR from understating respiratory/effort load when breathing is interrupted or transformed by functional respiratory behaviours.

Pranaclimb signalMeaning
Raw BRWhat we actually observe.
Breath ExpressionsWhat happens to/through that respiratory pattern.
Effective BRPranaclimb's derived field intensity signal.

Raw BR is measured. Breath Expressions are observed and classified. Effective BR is calculated.

Importantly, neither Chen nor Aliverti validates the numerical Pranaclimb Breath Expression coefficients. Their research instead strengthens the physiological rationale for why Pranaclimb distinguishes Raw BR from the respiratory expressions occurring within that breathing pattern. The coefficients themselves remain operational parameters awaiting prospective validation.

So Why Do Climbers Power Scream?

Because the scream may be doing several things at once. It can be emotional. It can focus attention. It can provide auditory feedback. It can couple expiration to movement. It can accompany abdominal pressure generation.

Experimental evidence suggests that vocalised exhalation can enhance acute force production, while forceful abdominal yelling during maximal exercise can alter respiratory and cardiorespiratory responses.

So the power scream isn't simply something we hear. It is something the respiratory system, abdominal muscles and moving body do.

Breath. Movement. Pressure. Power. Expression.

And sometimes, when the move demands everything: we scream.
