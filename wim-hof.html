Wim Hof–Style Breathing

Rhythmic deep breathing, a relaxed low-lung-volume retention and one recovery breath—used off the wall to rehearse composure during strong respiratory sensations.

Pranaclimb Position

How Climbers Can Use This Technique

Pranaclimb uses Wim Hof–style breathing as a powerful off-wall activation and controlled-stress intervention. The deliberate shift from rhythmic deep breathing into retention creates strong changes in respiratory sensation. Practised progressively, it provides a structured way to develop readiness, focus, volitional control and a composed return from respiratory discomfort—qualities directly relevant to committing under pressure in hard climbing.
Mechanism & Coaching

The Breathing Science

  • Rhythmic deep breathing increases ventilation, lowers arterial CO₂ and temporarily raises blood pH, creating a distinct state of physiological activation.
  • Low-lung-volume retention follows a relaxed exhale. Oxygen then falls and CO₂ begins to rebuild, creating a controlled hypoxic and interoceptive challenge.
  • The recovery breath completes the cycle. A deliberate inhale and brief hold restore ventilation and rehearse the transition from respiratory challenge back into control.
One Complete Round

How To Complete The First Round

  • 30–40 circular breaths: inhale fully into the belly and then the chest. Let the air go unforced through the mouth and begin the next inhale without pausing.
  • Guided retention: after the final relaxed exhale, hold at low lung volume for the selected 30-second, 1-minute, 1½-minute or 2-minute interval.
  • 15-second recovery hold: take one full recovery inhale, hold it for 15 seconds, then release. This completes one round.
Exact Intervention

Choose Your Practice Level

Beginner

3 rounds

30 rhythmic breaths followed by a guided 30-second or 1-minute retention. Complete each round with the 15-second recovery hold.

Medium

4 rounds

30–35 rhythmic breaths followed by a guided 1-minute or 1½-minute retention. Use four rounds once the basic cycle feels familiar and controlled.

Advanced

4 rounds

40 rhythmic breaths followed by a guided 1½-minute or 2-minute retention. Complete every round with a full inhale and 15-second recovery hold.

Timing

When To Use It For Climbing

  • Before an off-wall training session when activation and focus are useful
  • As a controlled interoceptive practice on a non-climbing day
  • As a repeatable preparation ritual when commitment and mental clarity matter
Field Measurement

What To Record

Record the practice level, number of rounds, selected and completed retention, pre/post RPE, perceived readiness and the return of natural breathing. Over repeated sessions, these measures create an individual profile of respiratory composure and voluntary control. Retention time is interpreted alongside the complete response rather than as a standalone performance score.

Research

Performance Science & Pranaclimb Application

Voluntary physiological control

Trained participants using meditation, cyclic hyperventilation and cold exposure produced a greater epinephrine response and altered the inflammatory response during experimental endotoxemia. The study demonstrates that deliberate practice can influence physiological systems previously considered largely automatic.

Kox et al., 2014 →
Exercise warm-up

In a small study of adolescent endurance athletes, a 17–22-minute Wim Hof–style breathing warm-up accelerated VO₂ kinetics and reduced subjective strain during incremental cycling. This provides an emerging performance rationale for pre-exercise respiratory priming.

Bahenský et al. →
Evidence synthesis

A systematic review found promising evidence for voluntary modulation of inflammatory responses, while exercise-performance results were mixed. For Pranaclimb, this supports confident use as an off-wall activation and controlled-stress practice while climbing-specific transfer remains an active field question.

Almahayni & Hammond, 2024 →

Safety Boundary

Practise In Control

Practise in a safe position: Remain seated or lying down. Never practise in water, in a bath or shower, while driving, standing, climbing or belaying. Select a guided retention appropriate to your experience and resume breathing sooner whenever your body clearly asks. Stop immediately for faintness, pain, confusion or distress. People who are pregnant or who have cardiovascular, neurological, respiratory or fainting conditions should seek appropriate clinical advice first.
