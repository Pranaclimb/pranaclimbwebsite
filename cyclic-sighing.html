Breathing for Climbers · Physiological Sigh · Emotion Regulation

Cyclic Sighing

A double nasal inhale followed by a relaxed, extended exhale. Use it to interrupt the momentum of stress, create a deliberate pause and return attention to the next climbing decision.

Evidence-Supported 1 Breath · 1 Minute · 5 Minutes Off-Wall or Secure Rest Pause · Release · Refocus

The breath pattern

Double Inhale. Relaxed Exhale.

Cyclic sighing is an exhale-focused breathing practice built from repeated physiological sighs: inhale through the nose, add a shorter second nasal inhale, then allow a slow, extended exhale through the mouth. The exhale should feel like a release—not a forced emptying of the lungs.

A sigh is both a respiratory event and a change-of-state signal. The second inhale further expands the lungs; then the inspiratory muscles relax and the air is allowed to leave slowly. That physical sense of letting go can create a clear psychological pause between the stress response and the climber’s next decision.

Pranaclimb position: Cyclic sighing is a practical, evidence-supported regulation and refocusing tool for climbers. One sigh can interrupt the momentum of stress, one minute can regulate arousal between attempts, and five minutes provides a structured pre-climb or recovery practice. The double inhale creates expansion; the relaxed extended exhale creates release—helping the climber return to calm, decision-ready breathing.

Step by step

How To Practise Cyclic Sighing

  1. SettleSit, stand or lie down somewhere safe. Soften the face, jaw and shoulders.
  2. First inhaleInhale gently through the nose, allowing the lower ribs to widen. Aim for comfortably full rather than maximal.
  3. Second inhaleWithout exhaling, add one shorter nasal sip of air.
  4. Relaxed extended exhaleSoften the breathing muscles and let the air leave slowly through the mouth. Guide the length of the exhale, but do not squeeze or strain at the end.
  5. Return or repeatFor an immediate reset, return to natural breathing after one cycle. For a structured intervention, continue comfortably for one or five minutes.

Pranaclimb interventions

Choose the Dose for the Moment

1 Breath

Pause & Refocus

Use: before leaving the ground, after a fright, or at a secure rest. Complete one comfortable double inhale and relaxed exhale, then return to unrestricted breathing.

1 Minute

Between Attempts

Use: when arousal is still high after lowering off. Repeat slowly for approximately 6–8 cycles, then notice whether attention and perceived tension have changed.

5 Minutes

Pre-Climb & Daily Practice

Use: seated or lying down before climbing, after training or as a daily practice. Five minutes a day was the dose used in the 2023 randomised trial.

Match the dose to the moment. During climbing, one well-timed sigh at a secure rest is usually enough; then allow breathing to respond freely to the movement.

Zone Mapping

When To Use Cyclic Sighing for Climbing

Before climbing — five minutes to settle attention and create a repeatable pre-performance routine
Between attempts — one breath or one minute to interrupt residual stress before analysis and recovery
Secure rest — one deliberate sigh, then resume the breathing rhythm required by the climbing
After climbing — five quiet minutes as a transition from performance to recovery

Field tracking: record pre/post emotional arousal or stress (0–10), respiratory rate and the time taken to regain calm, decision-ready breathing. HRR₆₀ can be recorded alongside the intervention under repeatable conditions to explore the individual recovery response.

Research Spotlight

Cyclic Sighing Science & Evidence

Positive Affect · Respiratory Rate · Randomised Trial

Five Minutes Daily: Mood Up, Breathing Rate Down

In the 28-day randomised trial led by Melis Yilmaz Balban and a Stanford research team that included Andrew Huberman, participants practised five minutes a day of cyclic sighing, box breathing, cyclic hyperventilation with retention, or breath-focused mindfulness. All four groups improved, but cyclic sighing produced the largest increase in positive affect and a greater reduction in resting respiratory rate than mindfulness in the reported analyses. This directly supports Pranaclimb’s five-minute pre-climb and recovery dose. The study measured mood and resting physiology rather than climbing performance.

Balban et al., 2023 · Cell Reports Medicine

Real-Life Pilot · Acute Anxiety

A One-Minute Option

A 2026 pilot ecological-momentary study compared one minute of cyclic sighing, box breathing and a control condition in everyday moments of anxiety. Both breathing practices were associated with greater acute anxiety reduction than control. This supports the short Pranaclimb “between attempts” option, while the pilot design means the result should be replicated.

Riedl et al., 2026 · Anxiety, Stress, & Coping

Mechanism · Dynamic Regulation

What Happens Across the Sigh Cycle

A 2026 laboratory study of fixed-interval volitional sighs found a brief cardiovascular mobilisation during the sigh, followed by recovery. Its protocol differed from Balban-style cyclic sighing, but it demonstrates that the practice is a dynamic regulatory sequence rather than a single autonomic switch. Pattern, frequency, context and the recovery after each sigh shape the response.

Muzumdar et al., 2026 · Psychophysiology

Practice boundary

Keep the Intervention Matched to the Climb

Use cyclic sighing off the wall or at a genuinely secure rest. Do not follow a timer while climbing, belaying, driving, swimming or in any situation requiring continuous attention. Keep the breaths comfortable. Stop and return to natural breathing if you feel dizzy, tingly, breathless or more unsettled. It is a self-regulation practice, not a replacement for professional support for persistent or severe anxiety.
