Breathing for Climbers · Emotion Regulation

Cyclic Sighing

A double nasal inhale followed by a relaxed, extended exhale. Use it to interrupt the momentum of stress, create a deliberate pause and return attention to the next climbing decision.

Evidence-Supported 1 Breath · 1 Minute · 5 Minutes Off-Wall or Secure Rest Pause · Release · Refocus

The breath pattern

Double Inhale. Relaxed Exhale.

Cyclic sighing is an exhale-focused breathing practice: inhale through the nose, add a shorter second nasal inhale, then allow a slow, extended exhale through the mouth. The exhale should feel like a release—not a forced emptying of the lungs.

A sigh is both a respiratory event and a change-of-state signal. The larger inhalation changes lung volume and cardiorespiratory dynamics; the longer exhale slows the rhythm and creates a distinct sensation of completion. This can provide the climber with a clear psychological pause between the stress response and the next action.

Pranaclimb position: Cyclic sighing is an evidence-supported regulation tool that combines a double inhale with a relaxed, extended exhale to interrupt the momentum of stress and create a clear psychological pause. Its autonomic effect is dynamic: each sigh briefly mobilises cardiorespiratory activity before the extended exhale supports release and settling. In practice, dose matters—one sigh can reset the moment, one minute can help regulate arousal between attempts, and five minutes provides a structured pre- or post-climb practice.

Step by step

How To Practise Cyclic Sighing

  1. SettleSit, stand or lie down somewhere safe. Soften the face, jaw and shoulders.
  2. First inhaleInhale gently through the nose, allowing the lower ribs to widen. Aim for comfortably full rather than maximal.
  3. Second inhaleWithout exhaling, add one shorter nasal sip of air.
  4. Relaxed extended exhaleRelease slowly through the mouth. Let the exhale be longer than the inhalation, but never squeeze or strain at the end.
  5. Return or repeatFor an immediate reset, return to natural breathing after one cycle. For a structured intervention, continue comfortably for one or five minutes.

Pranaclimb interventions

Choose the Dose for the Moment

1 Breath

Pause & Refocus

Use: before leaving the ground, after a fright, or at a secure rest. Complete one comfortable double inhale and relaxed exhale, then return to unrestricted breathing.

1 Minute

Between Attempts

Use: when arousal is still high after lowering off. Repeat slowly for approximately 6–8 cycles, then notice whether attention and perceived tension have changed.

5 Minutes

Daily Practice

Use: seated or lying down before climbing, after training or as a daily practice. This is the duration used in the 2023 month-long comparative trial.

Do not impose continuous cyclic sighing through hard climbing. During movement, use the smallest intervention that restores control—often one sigh—then allow breathing to respond freely to the task.

Zone Mapping

When To Use Cyclic Sighing for Climbing

Before climbing — five minutes to settle attention and create a repeatable pre-performance routine
Between attempts — one breath or one minute to interrupt residual stress before analysis and recovery
Secure rest — one deliberate sigh, then resume the breathing rhythm required by the climbing
After climbing — five quiet minutes as a transition from performance to recovery

Field tracking: record pre/post emotional arousal or stress (0–10), respiratory rate and the time taken to regain calm, decision-ready breathing. HRR₆₀ may be recorded alongside the intervention, but cyclic sighing should not be credited with improving HRR₆₀ unless the change is measured under repeatable conditions.

Research Spotlight

Cyclic Sighing Science & Evidence

Mood · Arousal · RCT

Five Minutes Daily for One Month

In a remote randomised controlled trial, three five-minute breathwork practices and breath-focused mindfulness were compared over one month. Breathwork—especially exhale-focused cyclic sighing—produced greater improvement in positive affect and a larger reduction in resting respiratory rate than mindfulness in the reported models. State anxiety and negative affect improved across the interventions; the trial did not establish cyclic sighing as a treatment for anxiety disorders.

Balban et al., 2023 · Cell Reports Medicine

Real-Life Pilot · Acute Anxiety

A One-Minute Option

A 2026 pilot ecological-momentary study compared one minute of cyclic sighing, box breathing and a control condition in everyday moments of anxiety. Both breathing practices were associated with greater acute anxiety reduction than control. This supports the short Pranaclimb “between attempts” option, while the pilot design means the result should be replicated.

Riedl et al., 2026 · Anxiety, Stress, & Coping

Replication Signal · Conference Poster

Physiology Improved; Mood Did Not Replicate

A 20-day University of Waterloo study reported lower resting respiratory rate and increased HRV in the cyclic-sighing group, but did not reproduce the earlier mood or anxiety findings. Because this evidence is currently available as a conference poster rather than a full peer-reviewed paper, it is informative—not definitive.

Boucher & Dixon, 2024 · N=167

Mechanism · Important Distinction

A Sigh Is Not Simply “Parasympathetic”

A 2026 laboratory study of fixed-interval volitional sighs found clear cardiovascular responses consistent with sympathetic activation, particularly when sighs were more frequent. Its protocol differed from Balban-style cyclic sighing, but it shows why the mechanism should not be described as a simple vagal switch. Pattern, frequency, context and the recovery after each sigh all matter.

Muzumdar et al., 2026 · Psychophysiology

Broader Context · Not Technique-Specific

Structured Breathing and Well-Being

A non-randomised 14-week programme in university students reported improvements in several well-being and sleep measures. The programme used diaphragmatic, pursed-lip and alternate-nostril breathing—not cyclic sighing—so it supports structured breathwork generally and must not be presented as direct evidence for this technique.

Crișan et al., 2025 · BMC Public Health

Practice boundary

Keep the Intervention Matched to the Climb

Use cyclic sighing off the wall or at a genuinely secure rest. Do not follow a timer while climbing, belaying, driving, swimming or in any situation requiring continuous attention. Keep the breaths comfortable. Stop and return to natural breathing if you feel dizzy, tingly, breathless or more unsettled. It is a self-regulation practice, not a replacement for professional support for persistent or severe anxiety.
